# AIMS Generic Bayesian Mechanism Inference and Evidence-Update Protocol

## Purpose

AIMS should produce a mechanism interpretation that is evidence-ranked, uncertainty-bounded, quantitatively calibrated, and updateable.

The goal is not to assign a single explanation by qualitative judgment, but to determine:

1. which mechanisms are currently favored;
2. which mechanisms remain plausible but incomplete;
3. which physical channels or model parameters control the observation;
4. which unmeasured variables, artifacts, or missing model classes could change the interpretation;
5. what experiment, characterization, theory calculation, or analysis would most reduce the remaining ambiguity.

AIMS may generate hypotheses, identify observables, select available tools, run model calibration and attribution workflows, and propose follow-up evidence. Posterior probabilities, Bayes factors, credible intervals, posterior predictive checks, robustness ranges, model-adequacy factors, and action-priority scores must be generated by code-based inference when quantitative tools are available. They must not be assigned by prose alone.

This protocol is generic. It should not assume any specific material system, mechanism, measurement modality, theory method, or conclusion.

---

## Core principle: uncertainty becomes action

AIMS must treat uncertainty as an operational signal, not only as an error bar.

For every important uncertainty identified during inference, AIMS must determine whether it should trigger one of the following actions:

1. **Measure** — acquire a new experimental observable.
2. **Map** — acquire spatial, temporal, or control-parameter dependence.
3. **Calibrate** — reduce instrumental, analysis, or parameter uncertainty.
4. **Characterize** — directly test a hidden sample, system, environmental, or artifact variable.
5. **Calculate** — run a theory, simulation, or model-discrepancy test.
6. **Generate hypothesis** — propose a new mechanism, secondary effect, artifact, or missing-model class.
7. **Update** — incorporate new evidence and revise the mechanism ranking.
8. **Stop** — declare the current evidence insufficient, not distinguishable, or not worth further action.

Uncertainty should therefore be reported as:

```text
uncertainty → action → new evidence → updated conclusion
```

AIMS must not end with a passive statement such as “more data are needed” when a concrete action can be proposed. Each major uncertainty should be converted into a specific measurement, calculation, calibration, characterization, or hypothesis-generation step.

---

## Run structure

This protocol is run as an evidence-updating loop.

### Initial run

AIMS analyzes all evidence currently available, including experimental data, extracted observables, analysis tools, simulation outputs, supplied references, and known limitations of the measurement or theory.

It begins from broad seed hypotheses, performs AIMS-led hypothesis expansion, freezes the hypothesis list for the current run, calibrates or marginalizes weakly constrained model parameters, performs Bayesian model comparison, evaluates model adequacy, performs mechanism attribution where possible, and proposes next evidence.

The initial report must include the evidence manifest, seed hypotheses, AIMS-generated hypotheses, frozen hypothesis list, current mechanism ranking, closed-model comparison, open-mechanism inference, convergence diagnostics, calibrated parameters, experimental error model, posterior predictive checks, model-adequacy assessment, uncertainty ledger, mechanism attribution, unmeasured-variable audit, and proposed next evidence.

### Update run

When new experimental, theoretical, computational, characterization, or analysis evidence is added, AIMS reruns the same protocol and reports how the mechanism ranking, model adequacy, calibrated parameters, physical interpretation, and remaining uncertainties changed.

Earlier reports should not be overwritten. The scientific result is the update.

AIMS must not treat unperformed future measurements or calculations as observed facts. Before such evidence exists, it may enter only as a latent variable, prior, model discrepancy, or proposed next step.

---

## 1. Pre-specification, provenance, and audit trail

Before each run, AIMS must record:

- protocol version;
- run type: initial or update;
- evidence available at run start;
- accessible tools;
- model versions;
- analysis-code versions;
- code commit hashes when available;
- random seeds;
- human-provided instructions;
- human-provided hypotheses, if any;
- human interventions during the run.

The report must distinguish:

1. pre-existing protocol rules;
2. user-supplied scientific goals;
3. user-supplied hypotheses;
4. AIMS-generated hypotheses;
5. tool-selected analyses;
6. post-hoc interpretations;
7. new evidence added after the initial run.

No hypothesis, likelihood, prior, or decision rule may be changed after observing the posterior unless the change is recorded as a new protocol version or update run.

AIMS must maintain an audit log of hypotheses proposed, hypotheses retained or rejected, tools selected and why, parameters calibrated or fixed, failed analyses, human interventions, and evidence added after the initial run.

---

## 2. Evidence manifest

Before inference, AIMS must create an evidence manifest listing:

- available experimental datasets;
- extracted observables;
- available analysis tools;
- available theory or simulation tools;
- supplied references;
- relevant quantities not yet directly measured;
- known measurement limitations;
- known model limitations.

Each item must be labeled as observed evidence, extracted quantity, computed model output, literature-supported prior knowledge, model assumption, latent variable, or proposed future evidence.

AIMS should use all relevant evidence currently available and should not ignore datasets, tools, or references without explanation.

---

## 3. Scientific question and seed hypotheses

AIMS begins from a scientific question and an initial seed set of hypotheses.

Seed hypotheses may come from:

1. human expert input;
2. supplied literature;
3. previous AIMS runs;
4. prior experimental observations;
5. available theory or simulation models;
6. AIMS-generated reasoning from the evidence manifest.

The seed list should be broad enough to cover the main plausible classes of explanation, but it is not assumed to be complete.

The seed list must include:

1. at least one leading mechanism proposed by the human, literature, prior analysis, or AIMS;
2. at least one plausible competing mechanism when available;
3. at least one sample-level, instrumental, analysis, hidden-variable, or artifact possibility when relevant;
4. a decision state: **not distinguishable with current evidence**.

“Not distinguishable” is a decision state, not a physical mechanism.

AIMS must clearly label the provenance of every seed hypothesis. Human-provided seeds are scientific inputs, not privileged answers. They must be tested against the evidence, challenged by alternatives, and updated in the same way as AIMS-generated hypotheses.

---

## 4. Human-provided seed hypotheses

A human scientist may provide seed hypotheses, candidate mechanisms, expected outcomes, known concerns, or preferred comparisons at the start of a run.

Human-provided seed hypotheses are allowed because expert intuition, prior experiments, and domain knowledge are part of scientific practice. However, they are starting points, not conclusions.

Human expert input of seed hypotheses include:
1. classical thermal melting;
2. quantum-fluctuation-influenced melting;

AIMS must record each human-provided seed hypothesis in the audit trail and label it as one of:

- **human-provided seed hypothesis**;
- **literature-derived seed hypothesis**;
- **prior-run hypothesis**;
- **AIMS-generated hypothesis**;
- **co-produced hypothesis**, if it arose through feedback during the run.

Human-provided seed hypotheses must not be given privileged status unless the human explicitly provides a justified prior. By default, they enter the hypothesis-generation stage as candidates to be tested, challenged, refined, merged, or rejected.

For each human-provided seed hypothesis, AIMS must create a seed-hypothesis card containing:

1. hypothesis name;
2. source: human, literature, prior run, AIMS, or mixed;
3. mechanism class: primary mechanism, secondary effect, artifact, hidden variable, model inadequacy, or decision state;
4. current evidence supporting it;
5. current evidence arguing against it;
6. observables it predicts or explains;
7. observables it may fail to explain;
8. competing hypotheses that could produce similar evidence;
9. most direct test;
10. whether it is rankable with current evidence, partially rankable, or latent.

AIMS must then perform an expansion and challenge step. For every human-provided seed hypothesis, AIMS must ask:

- What alternative mechanism could explain the same observable?
- What hidden variable or artifact could mimic this hypothesis?
- What model inadequacy could make this hypothesis appear favored?
- What observation would falsify or weaken it?
- What measurement, characterization, calculation, or calibration would most distinguish it from alternatives?

AIMS must include at least one plausible competing hypothesis or decision state unless current evidence makes alternatives physically irrelevant. If no plausible alternative exists, AIMS must state why.

AIMS must not suppress AIMS-generated alternatives because they complicate, weaken, or compete with a human-provided seed hypothesis. Retain any alternative that changes the mechanism ranking, uncertainty ledger, evidence plan, or decision label.

---

## 5. Priors for human-provided hypotheses

Human-provided hypotheses may receive prior weights only if the human supplies a scientific justification, such as previous measurements, literature evidence, known device history, or established physical constraints.

If no explicit justification is provided, human-provided hypotheses should not receive extra prior weight merely because they came from a human.

When expert priors are used, AIMS must report:

1. who or what supplied the prior;
2. the justification for the prior;
3. the numerical prior weight or prior distribution;
4. a sensitivity test using weaker, equal, or alternative priors.

If the mechanism ranking depends strongly on the human-provided prior, the result must be labeled **prior-sensitive**.

---

## 6. Uncertainty ledger

Each report must include an uncertainty ledger.

For every major uncertainty, AIMS must record:

- **source:** measurement, observable extraction, model parameter, model inadequacy, hidden variable, artifact, hypothesis-space gap, or evidence conflict;
- **affected claim:** which mechanism ranking, physical interpretation, observable, or action depends on it;
- **severity:** minor, moderate, major, or blocking;
- **rankability:** whether it can be included in the current Bayesian ranking, only bounded, or only treated as latent;
- **action:** measure, map, calibrate, characterize, calculate, generate hypothesis, update, or stop;
- **expected contrast:** what different hypotheses predict for the proposed action;
- **decision impact:** how the result would change the mechanism ranking, model adequacy, or confidence label.

AIMS should prioritize uncertainties that are both scientifically consequential and actionable. Low-impact uncertainties may be documented without immediate action. Major or blocking uncertainties must be converted into next evidence unless no feasible action exists.

---

## 7. AIMS-led hypothesis generation

After inspecting the evidence manifest and uncertainty ledger, AIMS must run a hypothesis-generation pass before Bayesian model comparison.

This pass is not optional. Its purpose is to prevent inference from being limited to the user-supplied model set.

### 7.1 Triggers for new hypotheses

AIMS should propose additional hypotheses when any of the following are present:

- failure of a seed model;
- unexplained residuals in key observables;
- mismatch in absolute scale, hierarchy, trend, sign, onset, spatial pattern, or reproducibility;
- dependence on extrapolated, censored, indirect, thresholded, or model-derived observables;
- disagreement between measurement modalities;
- spatial, temporal, field, frequency, temperature, dose, concentration, or control-parameter trends;
- sample-level, device-level, environmental, or instrumental variables that could plausibly affect the interpretation;
- mechanisms discussed in supplied references but absent from the seed list;
- limitations of the available theory, simulation, approximation, or finite-size treatment;
- known artifacts or calibration uncertainties of the measurement modality;
- unexplained success of a model for one observable but failure for another.

AIMS should convert each important model failure or residual into at least one possible physical, instrumental, or model-inadequacy explanation.

AIMS must not leave “model discrepancy,” “disorder,” “artifact,” “inhomogeneity,” or “unknown mechanism” as abstract labels if they can be decomposed into concrete testable variables.

### 7.2 Hypothesis search axes

AIMS should search for hypotheses along the following axes:

1. **Primary mechanism alternatives** — mechanisms that could explain the main observable.
2. **Secondary corrections** — effects that may not dominate, but could modify scale, trend, hierarchy, spatial pattern, or reproducibility.
3. **Mixed mechanisms** — cases where two or more mechanisms plausibly control different observables or regimes.
4. **Model-inadequacy mechanisms** — missing theory, approximation failure, finite-size limitation, coarse-graining error, missing interaction terms, neglected dynamics, or invalid parameter transfer.
5. **Hidden sample or system variables** — unmeasured structure, morphology, disorder, environment, local composition, geometry, boundary condition, strain, thickness, doping, concentration, temperature, aging, contamination, or other system-specific variables.
6. **Measurement or analysis artifacts** — calibration error, normalization artifact, signal cross-talk, drift, saturation, thresholding, selection bias, segmentation error, registration error, fitting artifact, or limited dynamic range.
7. **Observable-definition alternatives** — possibility that the extracted quantity is not the intended physical quantity, or that a proxy observable mixes multiple effects.
8. **Open mechanism category** — a decision state representing plausible missing mechanisms not implemented in the current model set.

### 7.3 Uncertainty-driven hypothesis generation

AIMS must use unresolved uncertainty as an input to hypothesis generation.

For each diagnostic residual, failed posterior predictive check, hidden variable, unexplained trend, model discrepancy, or indirect/censored observable, AIMS must ask:

1. What mechanism could make this uncertainty physically meaningful?
2. What secondary effect or hidden variable could mimic the leading mechanism?
3. What model approximation or missing model class could explain the residual?
4. What artifact or observable-definition problem could create the same pattern?
5. What measurement or calculation would distinguish these possibilities?

AIMS-generated hypotheses should arise from specific uncertainties, not from generic brainstorming.

Each retained hypothesis must be linked to at least one uncertainty in the uncertainty ledger and at least one proposed action. A hypothesis that cannot be connected to a data feature, residual, hidden variable, model limitation, literature mechanism, or concrete test should not be retained.

### 7.4 Adversarial hypothesis generation

AIMS must explicitly challenge the leading hypothesis.

For the current leading explanation, AIMS should ask:

- What observation would be hardest for this explanation to account for?
- What plausible mechanism could explain that observation instead?
- Is that alternative already included?
- If not, should it be added as a rankable hypothesis, latent hypothesis, artifact possibility, or \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\) contributor?
- What evidence would distinguish the two?

This adversarial pass should not be used to invent unsupported alternatives. It should be used to identify plausible missing explanations grounded in data, residuals, references, measurement limits, theory limits, or system configuration.

### 7.5 Hypothesis cards

For every AIMS-generated hypothesis, AIMS must create a hypothesis card containing:

- hypothesis name;
- mechanism class: primary, secondary, mixed, artifact, hidden variable, model inadequacy, or open decision state;
- why it was generated;
- data feature, residual, literature source, physical argument, or model failure motivating it;
- observables it could affect;
- predicted direction or pattern when possible;
- current evidence supporting it;
- current evidence arguing against it;
- whether it is currently rankable, partially rankable, or latent;
- what model, calculation, or likelihood would be needed to rank it quantitatively;
- most direct experiment, characterization, or calculation that would test it;
- expected outcome if the hypothesis is true;
- expected outcome if the leading competing hypothesis is true.

### 7.6 Hypothesis retention filter

AIMS should not add hypotheses for completeness alone.

A new hypothesis should be retained only if it changes at least one of the following:

- mechanism ranking;
- model adequacy assessment;
- dominant uncertainty;
- interpretation of a model failure;
- proposed next experiment;
- proposed next theory calculation;
- treatment of a key observable;
- classification of the result as resolved, favored, favored but incomplete, mixed, not distinguishable, or evidence insufficient.

Each rejected candidate hypothesis should be listed briefly with the reason for rejection when it was considered but not retained.

### 7.7 Frozen hypothesis list

Before Bayesian model comparison, AIMS must freeze the hypothesis list for the current run.

The frozen list must distinguish seed hypotheses, AIMS-generated hypotheses, rankable hypotheses, latent hypotheses, artifact possibilities, model-inadequacy hypotheses, and open decision states.

If new evidence later motivates new hypotheses, they may be added only in an update run, with the reason for addition explicitly recorded.

---

## 8. Observables and evidence labels

AIMS should use all relevant observables available in the current evidence state.

Each observable must be labeled as directly measured, extracted, indirectly inferred, model-dependent, extrapolated, censored, thresholded, literature-derived, or latent.

If an observable is indirect, extrapolated, censored, thresholded, or model-derived, the likelihood must reflect the actual information content. Such quantities must not be treated as direct Gaussian measurements unless justified.

For censored or bounded observables, AIMS should use a censored likelihood, survival likelihood, interval likelihood, bound likelihood, ordinal likelihood, or another appropriate evidence model.

Extrapolated values may be shown for visualization, but the primary inference should use the directly supported information.

---

## 9. Experimental error model

AIMS must explicitly separate experimental measurement error from model discrepancy.

For every observable \(O_k\), AIMS must construct an experimental error budget including all applicable terms:

\[
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{exp}}^2
=
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{noise}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{repeat}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{extract}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{cal}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{drift}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{reg}}^2
+
\sigma_{k,\mathrm{sys}}^2 .
\]

Here the terms represent instrumental noise, repeatability, observable-extraction uncertainty, calibration uncertainty, drift, spatial-registration uncertainty, and systematic experimental uncertainty.

If errors are correlated across observables, AIMS must use a covariance matrix rather than independent scalar errors:

\[
D_{\mathrm{obs}}
\sim
\mathcal{N}
\left(
D_{\mathrm{model}} + b,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{exp}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{model}}
\right).
\]

The bias term \(b\) represents systematic experimental offsets and should be fixed, fitted, bounded, or marginalized with a physically justified prior.

AIMS must not absorb experimental error into model discrepancy without explanation. Experimental uncertainty, extraction uncertainty, calibration uncertainty, and model discrepancy should be reported separately whenever possible.

For each observable, AIMS must report:

1. direct measurement or extracted quantity;
2. number of repeats or independent measurements;
3. noise or repeatability estimate;
4. extraction method and extraction uncertainty;
5. calibration uncertainty;
6. systematic or drift uncertainty;
7. whether the observable is direct, indirect, extrapolated, censored, thresholded, or model-derived;
8. how the uncertainty enters the likelihood.

AIMS must run sensitivity tests showing whether the mechanism ranking changes when experimental error bars are expanded, reduced, correlated, or replaced by alternative extraction pipelines.

## 10. Closed-model comparison, mechanism support, and remaining uncertainty

AIMS must distinguish four related but different quantities:

1. **closed-model comparison**;
2. **mechanism support**;
3. **alternative-mechanism uncertainty**;
4. **model-closure uncertainty**.

AIMS must not place all unresolved evidence into a single open category. A single unresolved category can be misleading when new evidence strengthens the favored mechanism but also reveals a more specific model limitation.

### 10.1 Closed-model comparison

Closed-model comparison asks which implemented model, among the models currently available to AIMS, best explains the selected observables.

Closed-model posterior probabilities may be reported as model-comparison weights, but they must be labeled as conditional on the implemented model set, chosen observables, priors, likelihood assumptions, model-discrepancy assumptions, calibration procedure, and available computational tools.

A high closed-model posterior means only that one implemented model is favored over the other implemented models. It does not by itself mean that the physical mechanism is resolved.

### 10.2 Mechanism support

Mechanism support asks:

> Which physical mechanism class is currently favored by the evidence?

Mechanism support should be based on all relevant evidence, including implemented model comparison, experimental trends, posterior predictive checks, robustness tests, direct characterization, and independent follow-up evidence.

A mechanism class may gain support when a model captures diagnostic features such as sign, trend, hierarchy, spatial pattern, response to a control parameter, or another mechanism-defining observable, even if the current implementation does not reproduce every observable quantitatively.

Mechanism support may therefore increase even when model-closure uncertainty remains high.

### 10.3 Alternative-mechanism uncertainty

Alternative-mechanism uncertainty asks:

> Could a different physical mechanism, hidden variable, artifact, or unimplemented mechanism class replace the favored interpretation?

Alternative-mechanism uncertainty receives support when plausible competing mechanisms, hidden variables, artifacts, or unimplemented mechanism classes could explain the key observables.

Alternative-mechanism uncertainty should decrease when direct evidence rules out, demotes, or reassigns competing explanations.

### 10.4 Model-closure uncertainty

Model-closure uncertainty asks:

> Is the favored mechanism class quantitatively closed by the current implemented model?

Model-closure uncertainty receives support when the favored mechanism class is likely correct, but the available model fails posterior predictive checks because of finite-size effects, approximation error, incomplete parameter scans, missing interaction terms, observable-definition uncertainty, extraction uncertainty, imperfect experimental criteria, or imperfect theoretical criteria.

Model-closure uncertainty is not evidence against the favored mechanism class unless the model failure specifically supports a plausible alternative mechanism.

A report must therefore state separately:

1. the favored mechanism class;
2. the remaining alternative-mechanism uncertainty;
3. the remaining model-closure uncertainty.

A decrease in alternative-mechanism uncertainty together with persistent model-closure uncertainty should be interpreted as scientific sharpening, not as weakening of the favored mechanism.

### 10.5 Two adequacy scores

For each implemented model, AIMS should report two adequacy scores.

#### Mechanism-class adequacy

\[
A^{\mathrm{class}}_i \in [0,1]
\]

measures whether model \(i\) captures the diagnostic features that identify the mechanism class. These may include qualitative hierarchy, sign, trend, onset, response to control parameters, spatial pattern, modality agreement, or another mechanism-defining observable.

A model can have high mechanism-class adequacy even if it does not quantitatively reproduce every observable.

#### Quantitative-closure adequacy

\[
A^{\mathrm{closure}}_i \in [0,1]
\]

measures whether model \(i\) quantitatively reproduces all key observables within uncertainty. This includes absolute scale, magnitude, posterior predictive pass rates, parameter convergence, finite-size convergence, model-discrepancy sensitivity, and robustness to extraction or criterion choices.

A model with high \(A^{\mathrm{class}}\) but low \(A^{\mathrm{closure}}\) supports the mechanism class but leaves quantitative theory incomplete.

The report must not collapse these two scores into one number.

### 10.6 Mechanism-support and uncertainty weighting

When quantitative tools permit, AIMS should report three quantities:

\[
P_{\mathrm{mech}}(H_i \mid D),
\]

\[
P_{\mathrm{alt}} \equiv P(\mathrm{alternative\ mechanism}\mid D),
\]

and

\[
U_{\mathrm{closure}} \equiv P(\mathrm{model\ closure\ incomplete}\mid D,H_{\mathrm{favored}}).
\]

Here, \(P_{\mathrm{mech}}(H_i \mid D)\) describes support for each physical mechanism class. \(P_{\mathrm{alt}}\) describes the probability or normalized support that a different mechanism, hidden variable, or artifact could replace the favored interpretation. \(U_{\mathrm{closure}}\) describes the remaining quantitative inadequacy of the current implementation within the favored mechanism class.

A default implementation is:

\[
\tilde{w}_i =
p(D\mid M_i)p(M_i)A^{\mathrm{class}}_i.
\]

The alternative-mechanism weight is:

\[
\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{alt}}
=
\sum_l \alpha_l S_l^{\mathrm{alt}},
\]

where \(S_l^{\mathrm{alt}}\) are severity scores for unresolved competing mechanisms, hidden variables, artifacts, or alternative interpretations.

The mechanism support is:

\[
P_{\mathrm{mech}}(H_i\mid D)
=
\frac{\tilde{w}_i}
{\sum_j \tilde{w}_j + \tilde{w}_{\mathrm{alt}}}.
\]

The alternative-mechanism uncertainty is:

\[
P_{\mathrm{alt}}
=
\frac{\tilde{w}_{\mathrm{alt}}}
{\sum_j \tilde{w}_j + \tilde{w}_{\mathrm{alt}}}.
\]

The model-closure uncertainty is reported separately:

\[
U_{\mathrm{closure}}
=
1-A^{\mathrm{closure}}_{\mathrm{favored}}.
\]

AIMS must not add \(U_{\mathrm{closure}}\) into \(P_{\mathrm{alt}}\) unless the quantitative model failure specifically supports a competing physical mechanism.

If this default implementation is not appropriate, AIMS must define and justify an alternative way of separating mechanism support, alternative-mechanism uncertainty, and model-closure uncertainty.

The report must never present closed-model posterior weights as final mechanism probabilities without this distinction.

## 11. Quantitative uncertainty-to-action layer

AIMS must convert each major uncertainty into a quantitative action trigger.

For every uncertainty \(u_j\), AIMS must report:

1. **uncertainty type:** measurement, extraction, parameter, model inadequacy, hidden variable, artifact, censored observable, hypothesis-space gap, or evidence conflict;
2. **affected claim:** mechanism ranking, model adequacy, physical interpretation, observable, or proposed action;
3. **uncertainty metric:** numerical quantity used to measure the uncertainty;
4. **severity score:** how strongly the uncertainty affects the scientific conclusion;
5. **actionability score:** how feasible and diagnostic the next action is;
6. **triggered action:** measure, map, calibrate, characterize, calculate, generate hypothesis, update, or stop.

### 11.1 Uncertainty severity

For each uncertainty \(u_j\), define a severity score:

\[
S_j = I_j R_j Q_j.
\]

Here:

- \(I_j\) is **claim impact**, scored from 0 to 1: how much the claim would change if this uncertainty were resolved.
- \(R_j\) is **ranking sensitivity**, scored from 0 to 1: how much the mechanism ranking changes under reasonable perturbations of this uncertainty.
- \(Q_j\) is **evidence-quality penalty**, scored from 0 to 1: how indirect, censored, extrapolated, model-dependent, weakly reproduced, or incomplete the relevant evidence is.

AIMS should classify:

\[
S_j < 0.2: \text{minor}
\]

\[
0.2 \leq S_j < 0.5: \text{moderate}
\]

\[
0.5 \leq S_j < 0.8: \text{major}
\]

\[
S_j \geq 0.8: \text{blocking}.
\]

Major or blocking uncertainties must trigger a concrete action unless no feasible action exists.

### 11.2 Model-adequacy factor

For each implemented model \(M_i\), AIMS must compute a model-adequacy factor:

\[
A_i \in [0,1].
\]

A default form is:

\[
A_i =
\left(\prod_k P_{\mathrm{PPC},k}^{w_k}\right)
R_i C_i.
\]

Here:

- \(P_{\mathrm{PPC},k}\) is the posterior predictive pass probability for key observable \(k\);
- \(w_k\) is the importance weight of observable \(k\);
- \(R_i\) is robustness under prior, likelihood, observable-inclusion, hidden-variable, and model-discrepancy perturbations;
- \(C_i\) is a convergence factor based on calibration and sampling diagnostics.

A high closed-model posterior with low \(A_i\) must be reported as **favored but incomplete**, not resolved.

### 11.3 Expected value of action

For each proposed action \(a\), AIMS must estimate an expected action value:

\[
V(a)
=
2EIG(a)
+
2C(a)
+
F(a)
-
K(a).
\]

Here:

- \(EIG(a)\) is expected information gain about the mechanism ranking or model adequacy;
- \(C(a)\) is diagnostic contrast between the leading hypotheses;
- \(F(a)\) is feasibility;
- \(K(a)\) is cost, time, or experimental burden.

If a full expected-information-gain calculation is unavailable, AIMS may estimate diagnostic contrast as:

\[
C(a)
=
\frac{
|\mu_1(a)-\mu_2(a)|
}{
\sqrt{\sigma_1^2(a)+\sigma_2^2(a)+\sigma_{\mathrm{meas}}^2}
}.
\]

Here, \(\mu_1(a)\) and \(\mu_2(a)\) are predicted outcomes under the top two hypotheses.

Actions with high \(S_j\) and high \(V(a)\) should be prioritized.

### 11.4 Required uncertainty-to-action table

Each report must include a table with the following columns:

| Uncertainty | Affected claim | Metric | Severity \(S_j\) | Triggered action | Expected contrast | Expected information gain | Feasibility | Priority | Expected update |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|

AIMS must explicitly identify the top three uncertainties by severity, the top three actions by expected value, which uncertainties triggered hypothesis generation, and which uncertainties triggered measurement, characterization, calculation, calibration, update, or stop decisions.

## 12. Model calibration before attribution

Before performing mechanism attribution with any theory, simulation, or fitting tool, AIMS must identify all free, fitted, nuisance, effective, or weakly constrained parameters that affect model predictions.

For each parameter, AIMS must state whether it is directly measured, independently constrained by calibration or literature, fitted to observables, marginalized over, scanned over, fixed by assumption, inherited from another model, or effective rather than microscopic.

AIMS must not fix an unconstrained parameter to a single arbitrary value.

If a parameter is not directly measured, AIMS must optimize it, marginalize over a physically reasonable prior, scan it and report sensitivity, or justify why fixing it is harmless.

If multiple free parameters are coupled, AIMS should optimize, sample, or marginalize them jointly.

Attribution analysis must be performed only after calibration, using accepted, optimized, high-likelihood, or posterior-weighted model trials.

If an available optimization, likelihood scan, posterior sample, or Bayesian-search workflow exists but is not used before attribution, the report must flag the attribution as incomplete and explain why the calibration step was skipped.

---

## 13. Quantitative calibration and convergence criteria

A small fixed grid is not sufficient unless followed by refinement or justified by convergence.

The default minimum standard is:

1. scan the full physically allowed range;
2. identify the top region by likelihood or lowest loss;
3. refine the search around that region or use Bayesian optimization, posterior sampling, or likelihood-guided adaptive search;
4. continue until convergence criteria are met.

If Bayesian optimization is used, AIMS should run at least 20 initial exploration points and 50 adaptive iterations for a two-parameter calibration problem, unless the calculation is too expensive.

For \(d\) calibrated parameters, the default minimum number of evaluations is:

\[
N_{\mathrm{eval}} \geq 20 + 20d.
\]

Calibration is considered converged only if the applicable criteria are met:

- best objective value improves by less than 1% over the final 20% of evaluations;
- posterior median or best-fit value of each calibrated parameter changes by less than 5% when the final 25% of samples or evaluations are removed;
- top-two mechanism ordering is unchanged under at least three optimizer seeds, bootstrap resamples, or posterior resamples;
- leading physical-channel attributions change by less than 10 percentage points across bootstrap resamples, posterior resamples, or independent surrogate fits;
- if posterior sampling is used, effective sample size for each calibrated parameter should exceed 200.

If the calculation cannot satisfy these criteria, AIMS must label the inference as partially converged, preliminary, or incomplete, and state whether the mechanism conclusion depends on the unconverged part.

AIMS must report calibrated parameters as posterior summaries or credible ranges, not only point estimates. The default summary is:

\[
\mathrm{median}\,[5\%,95\%].
\]

If an optimum lies within 10% of a prior boundary, AIMS must expand the range if physically allowed, or state why the boundary is physically meaningful.

## 14. Bayesian model comparison

For each rankable hypothesis \(H_i\), compute:

\[
p(H_i \mid D)
=
\frac{p(D \mid H_i)p(H_i)}
{\sum_j p(D \mid H_j)p(H_j)}.
\]

For each hypothesis, define model parameters, physically justified priors, model predictions, experimental uncertainty, model-discrepancy terms, likelihood, and model evidence.

The model evidence is:

\[
p(D \mid H_i)
=
\int p(D \mid \theta_i,H_i)
p(\theta_i \mid H_i)
d\theta_i.
\]

The likelihood should include measurement uncertainty and model discrepancy:

\[
y_k^{\mathrm{obs}}
=
y_{i,k}^{\mathrm{model}}(\theta_i)
+
\delta_{i,k}
+
\epsilon_k.
\]

Here, \(\epsilon_k\) is measurement uncertainty and \(\delta_{i,k}\) accounts for model inadequacy, approximation bias, finite-size effects, limited measurement range, normalization uncertainty, imperfect knowledge of hidden variables, or other model-data mismatch.

A model should be penalized if it fails to reproduce the key observables that motivated the mechanism question.

If the model comparison is closed over only a subset of plausible mechanisms, the report must label the posterior as closed-model evidence and separately report open-mechanism inference.

---

## 15. Model adequacy and posterior predictive checks

A high posterior weight is not sufficient if the model fails posterior predictive checks.

For each hypothesis or implemented model, AIMS must report whether posterior samples reproduce the key observables, including absolute scale, qualitative hierarchy, trend direction, sign, onset, threshold, spatial pattern, reproducibility, modality agreement, response to control parameters, or other diagnostic features relevant to the scientific question.

For each key observable \(O_k\), AIMS should compute a posterior predictive pass probability:

\[
P_{\mathrm{PPC},k}(H_i)
=
P[O_k^{\mathrm{model}} \in O_k^{\mathrm{allowed}} \mid D,H_i].
\]

AIMS should then report which checks pass, which fail, and which are inconclusive.

AIMS must separate posterior predictive checks into two classes:

1. **Mechanism-class checks** — checks that test whether the model captures the diagnostic features defining the mechanism class, such as sign, trend, hierarchy, control-parameter response, spatial pattern, or qualitative regime structure.
2. **Quantitative-closure checks** — checks that test whether the model quantitatively reproduces all key observables, including absolute magnitude, scale, threshold values, calibrated parameters, finite-size stability, and extraction-criterion robustness.

AIMS should use these checks to compute or justify:

\[
A^{\mathrm{class}}_i
\]

and

\[
A^{\mathrm{closure}}_i.
\]

If the leading model has high mechanism-class adequacy but low quantitative-closure adequacy, AIMS should classify the mechanism as favored while separately reporting model-closure uncertainty.

If the leading model has high closed-model posterior support but fails mechanism-class checks, AIMS must classify the physical interpretation as mixed, not distinguishable, or evidence insufficient. It must not call the mechanism favored or resolved.

AIMS should test whether the conclusion depends on model priors, parameter priors, model-discrepancy assumptions, inclusion or removal of key observables, treatment of indirect/censored/model-derived quantities, prior weight assigned to AIMS-generated hypotheses, calibration of weakly constrained parameters, hidden-variable assumptions, and experimental error budgets.

The report should state which conclusions are robust, which remain sensitive, and whether the sensitivity affects mechanism support, alternative-mechanism uncertainty, or model-closure uncertainty.

---

## 16. Physics-parameter attribution and mechanism decomposition

Whenever an available theory, simulation, or fitting tool maps physical inputs to measured observables, AIMS should perform parameter attribution if the tool output supports it.

The goal is to determine which physical channels control the observed behavior within the physically supported parameter region, not to vary one microscopic parameter in an unphysical way while holding correlated parameters fixed.

AIMS should identify external or experimentally meaningful input knobs, derived physical quantities, target observables, and the loss, likelihood, or posterior defining the physically relevant region.

When appropriate, AIMS should fit a surrogate model, convert accepted trials into derived physical features, group correlated features into physical channels, compute grouped attribution scores, estimate uncertainty, and report whether each channel controls absolute scale, hierarchy, trend, spatial pattern, or another diagnostic feature.

The output must include physical input knobs, derived physical features, grouping into physical channels, attribution method, contribution score and uncertainty for each channel, physical interpretation, and a figure or table summarizing channel contributions.

---

## 17. Unmeasured-variable and hidden-variable audit

For any experiment involving local probes, spatial inhomogeneity, fabricated devices, sample preparation, environmental sensitivity, model-data mismatch, or indirect observables, AIMS must identify unmeasured physical variables that could affect the interpretation.

AIMS should not leave broad explanations such as disorder, inhomogeneity, artifact, hidden variable, or model discrepancy as abstract labels.

It must decompose them into concrete variables implied by device or sample geometry, fabrication or preparation process, measurement modality, observed residuals, spatial pattern, temporal or environmental drift, supplied references, theory limitations, or known artifacts.

For each unmeasured variable, AIMS must report why it is physically plausible, whether current evidence supports or disfavors it, what claims it could affect, whether it should enter the Bayesian ranking or remain latent, the most direct test, and the expected outcomes under competing explanations.

AIMS should not claim that an unmeasured variable is active without evidence. If current data cannot test a physically plausible variable that could change the interpretation, AIMS must explicitly name it as unresolved and propose direct characterization.

### 17.1 Direct characterization of spatial or sample-level uncertainty

If AIMS identifies a spatially varying hidden variable, sample-structure variable, morphology variable, local disorder variable, strain-like variable, domain variable, thickness variable, reconstruction variable, composition variable, environmental variable, boundary variable, or fabrication-related variable that could affect any measured observable, AIMS must not absorb it only into an effective model parameter unless it explains why direct characterization is unnecessary.

For each such variable, AIMS must decide whether it affects:

1. the primary mechanism;
2. a secondary observable such as spatial amplitude, onset, linewidth, reproducibility, local strength, transition sharpness, or sample-to-sample variability;
3. only calibration or nuisance parameters;
4. no relevant observable.

If the variable is spatial, structural, compositional, morphological, or environmental and has not been directly characterized, AIMS must include a hidden-variable or secondary-discovery follow-up using the most direct feasible characterization method. Examples include topography, morphology mapping, structural imaging, strain mapping, compositional mapping, local spectroscopy, registered multimodal imaging, environmental mapping, or other system-appropriate characterization.

This follow-up must be listed even if the variable is not expected to replace the favored primary mechanism, provided it could explain spatial heterogeneity, sample selection, signal amplitude, reproducibility, transition sharpness, or another scientifically meaningful observable.

AIMS may choose an indirect proxy only if it explains why the proxy is more diagnostic than direct characterization and states what uncertainty remains.

### 17.2 Secondary-mechanism retention and follow-up

AIMS must not discard a secondary mechanism merely because it does not replace the favored primary mechanism.

If an uncertainty, hidden variable, artifact possibility, sample/system variable, or environmental variable is found to affect any important observable, AIMS must classify its role as one of:

1. **primary-mechanism driver** — controls the main phenomenon;
2. **secondary mechanism** — affects a different observable, regime, amplitude, spatial pattern, onset, linewidth, reproducibility, local strength, or sample selection;
3. **nuisance variable** — affects calibration or measurement but not the physical interpretation;
4. **inactive or ruled out** — tested and found not to affect relevant observables.

If a variable is demoted from primary-mechanism driver to secondary mechanism, AIMS must still retain it in the report if it explains an observable, exposes sample physics, or creates a new actionable question.

For each retained secondary mechanism, AIMS must report:

- which observable it controls;
- why it does not replace the primary mechanism;
- what evidence supports its secondary role;
- what direct follow-up would identify its microscopic origin, mechanism of action, or boundary of validity;
- whether this follow-up is needed for the main mechanism, for a secondary discovery, for reproducibility, or for future sample/device optimization.

A secondary mechanism must trigger a follow-up action when:

1. it is directly observed or statistically supported;
2. it affects a scientifically meaningful observable;
3. its microscopic origin remains unresolved;
4. it could guide future experiments, sample selection, device design, or interpretation of spatial heterogeneity.

The follow-up should be listed even if it has lower priority than the primary-mechanism or model-closure tests.

---

## 18. Direct-test requirement

AIMS must not leave a named hidden variable, secondary mechanism, or artifact possibility with only an indirect test if a direct characterization is feasible.

For each hidden variable or secondary mechanism, AIMS must identify:

- the most direct measurement or characterization of that variable;
- the most feasible indirect proxy, if different;
- which should be performed first and why;
- what claim would change if the variable were confirmed, demoted, or ruled out.

If the variable is structural, spatial, compositional, environmental, geometric, morphological, strain-like, domain-like, or calibration-related, AIMS should consider direct characterization of that variable rather than only absorbing it into an effective model parameter.

If AIMS chooses an indirect proxy instead of direct characterization, it must explain why the proxy is more diagnostic and state what uncertainty remains unresolved.

---

## 19. Decision rule

AIMS must assign two decision labels: a **mechanism label** and a **model-closure label**.

### 19.1 Mechanism label

The mechanism label describes the physical interpretation:

1. **mechanism rejected;**
2. **mechanism disfavored;**
3. **mechanism plausible;**
4. **mechanism favored;**
5. **mechanism resolved.**

A mechanism may be labeled **favored** when it has strong mechanism support, competing mechanisms are disfavored or demoted, and the main diagnostic features are reproduced or independently supported.

A mechanism may be labeled **resolved** only if:

- the leading mechanism has high mechanism support;
- alternative-mechanism uncertainty is low;
- the model passes mechanism-class posterior predictive checks;
- the conclusion remains stable under robustness tests;
- it does not rely critically on indirect, extrapolated, censored, or thresholded observables without proper likelihood treatment;
- it does not change under reasonable experimental-error budgets, alternative extraction choices, or calibration uncertainties;
- plausible hidden variables and artifacts have been tested or shown to be irrelevant.

### 19.2 Model-closure label

The model-closure label describes whether the current implementation quantitatively closes the evidence:

1. **not modeled;**
2. **preliminary;**
3. **partially closed;**
4. **mostly closed;**
5. **closed.**

A model may be labeled **closed** only if it quantitatively reproduces all key observables within uncertainty, passes quantitative-closure posterior predictive checks, has converged calibrated parameters, is stable under finite-size or approximation checks when relevant, and is robust to extraction or criterion choices.

### 19.3 Combined conclusion

The final conclusion should combine both labels.

Examples:

- **Mechanism favored; model partially closed.**
- **Mechanism resolved; model mostly closed.**
- **Mechanism plausible; model not yet closed.**
- **Mechanism favored; alternative mechanisms remain active.**

If the leading model has high closed-model posterior support but low quantitative-closure adequacy, the conclusion should not be weakened automatically at the mechanism level. Instead, AIMS must state whether the failure reflects alternative-mechanism uncertainty or model-closure uncertainty.

The decision must explain the physical reason for the classification, not only the numerical ranking.

---

## 20. Mandatory hypothesis-to-evidence conversion

AIMS must not end the mechanism-inference report with only a posterior ranking.

If any mechanism remains plausible, mixed, not distinguishable, sensitive to model assumptions, affected by alternative-mechanism uncertainty, or limited by model-closure uncertainty, AIMS must convert the remaining uncertainty into proposed evidence.

For each of the top competing or unresolved hypotheses, AIMS must identify the specific uncertainty preventing a stronger conclusion, whether the missing evidence is experimental/theoretical/computational/characterization-based/analysis-based, one feasible experiment or characterization, one feasible theory/simulation/analysis calculation, expected outcomes, and how the result would update the mechanism ranking.

Generic statements such as “more data are needed” or “better theory is needed” are not acceptable.

---

## 21. Separated follow-up evidence portfolio

AIMS must not rank all follow-up evidence in a single undifferentiated list. The report must separate proposed next evidence into four decision classes when applicable.

### 21.0 Minimum follow-up evidence portfolio

AIMS must preserve follow-up diversity across active uncertainty classes. The final evidence portfolio should include the highest-value action in each active uncertainty class, not only the highest-value actions overall.

One follow-up class must not replace another. A primary-mechanism experiment cannot replace a theory/model-closure calculation. A theory/model-closure calculation cannot replace a hidden-variable or spatial-characterization test. A spatial/sample-characterization test cannot replace a primary-mechanism discriminator.

If the leading mechanism is experimentally unresolved, observable-sensitive, or dependent on a key measurement that is indirect, censored, extrapolated, thresholded, or weakly measured, AIMS must include at least one **primary-mechanism experimental test**.

If the mechanism class is favored but the implemented model is not quantitatively closed, fails posterior predictive checks, has unconverged parameters, is finite-size limited, uses an approximate solver, relies on an unstable extraction criterion, or misses one or more key observables, AIMS must include at least one **theory/model-closure follow-up**.

If a hidden variable, spatial variable, sample-structure variable, morphology variable, compositional variable, environmental variable, artifact, or calibration uncertainty remains active, AIMS must include at least one **direct characterization or calibration follow-up**.

If a secondary mechanism is directly observed or statistically supported and controls a meaningful observable, AIMS must include at least one **secondary-discovery follow-up**, unless its microscopic origin and effect size are already established.

AIMS may rank actions within each class, but it must not drop an entire active class because another class has a higher total priority score. A lower-priority follow-up must remain in the report if it is the highest-priority action within its uncertainty class.

AIMS may omit a class only if it explicitly states that the corresponding uncertainty class is inactive, irrelevant, already resolved, or infeasible to test, and explains why.

### A. Primary-mechanism test

This follow-up directly distinguishes the leading physical mechanism from the main alternatives.

Report the experiment, characterization, observable, or perturbation; mechanisms distinguished; expected outcome under each mechanism; diagnostic contrast; feasibility; and expected posterior impact.

### B. Theory or model-closure test

This follow-up targets theoretical, computational, or model-closure uncertainty, especially when the leading mechanism class is favored but the implemented model misses scale, hierarchy, parameter dependence, dynamics, spatial pattern, or another key observable.

A theory/model-closure follow-up is required when any of the following are active: failed posterior predictive checks, finite-size limitation, approximation error, unstable parameter transfer, incomplete parameter scan, boundary-limited calibration, missing interaction terms, missing dynamical processes, solver convergence issues, extraction-criterion dependence, or disagreement between models within the same mechanism class.

The follow-up should identify the model limitation and propose one or more of: exact or beyond-approximation calculation, finite-size scaling, parameter scan or marginalization, model-discrepancy calculation, alternative observable or criterion, extended interaction or missing-term test, refined effective model, or convergence check across geometry, cluster, grid, basis, or solver settings.

This follow-up must remain in the separated evidence portfolio even if a primary-mechanism or hidden-variable follow-up has a higher total priority score.

Report the calculation, approximation or discrepancy tested, parameters to compute or scan, finite-size/convergence/transferability checks, expected outcome under each mechanism, and how the result changes mechanism support, alternative-mechanism uncertainty, or model-closure uncertainty in the next update.

### C. Hidden-variable or artifact test

This follow-up directly tests the most important unmeasured variable, sample variable, device variable, environmental variable, artifact possibility, or calibration uncertainty invoked in the analysis.

A hidden-variable or artifact follow-up is required when AIMS identifies a spatial, structural, compositional, environmental, calibration, morphology, strain-like, disorder, domain, reconstruction, contact, thickness, contamination, drift, registration, or observable-definition variable that could affect any important observable.

This follow-up should use the most direct feasible characterization or calibration method. Examples include topography, morphology mapping, structural imaging, strain mapping, compositional mapping, local spectroscopy, registered multimodal imaging, calibration standards, forward/inverse instrument modeling, or repeated measurements under controlled perturbations.

Report the hidden variable or artifact possibility, why it is plausible, whether current evidence supports or disfavors it, most direct characterization or calculation, expected outcomes, and how the result changes or does not change the ranking.

This test must be reported even if the variable is judged secondary and even if another follow-up has higher expected information gain for the primary mechanism. It must not be dropped because a theory/model-closure follow-up is higher priority.

### D. Secondary-discovery follow-up

This follow-up is required when AIMS identifies a secondary mechanism that does not replace the leading mechanism but controls another important observable.

Report:

- secondary mechanism;
- observable or regime it controls;
- evidence supporting its role;
- why it is secondary rather than primary;
- unresolved microscopic origin;
- direct measurement, characterization, perturbation, or calculation that would test it;
- expected outcome if the secondary mechanism is active;
- expected outcome if it is only a proxy or correlation;
- how the result would affect future measurements, reproducibility, sample selection, device design, or interpretation of spatial heterogeneity.

This class prevents AIMS from dropping discoveries that are not the main mechanism but are still scientifically valuable.

---

## 22. Quantitative follow-up scoring

Each proposed follow-up should be scored on three 1–5 scales:

1. **Diagnostic contrast:** how differently the leading hypotheses predict the outcome.
2. **Feasibility:** how practical the measurement, characterization, analysis, or calculation is with available capabilities.
3. **Expected posterior impact:** how much the result is expected to change mechanism support, alternative-mechanism uncertainty, model-closure uncertainty, secondary-mechanism assignment, or model-adequacy classification.

The total priority score is:

\[
S_{\mathrm{priority}}
=
2S_{\mathrm{contrast}}
+
S_{\mathrm{feasibility}}
+
2S_{\mathrm{impact}}.
\]

If a full expected-information-gain calculation is unavailable, AIMS should estimate diagnostic contrast using:

\[
C(E)
=
\frac{
|\mu_1(E)-\mu_2(E)|
}{
\sqrt{\sigma_1^2(E)+\sigma_2^2(E)+\sigma_{\mathrm{meas}}^2}
}.
\]

Here, \(\mu_1(E)\) and \(\mu_2(E)\) are predicted outcomes under the top two hypotheses.

A follow-up with \(C>2\) is strongly diagnostic, \(1<C<2\) is moderately diagnostic, and \(C<1\) is weakly diagnostic.

---

## 23. Discovery criterion

AIMS produces a discovery-level output when it does more than rank existing explanations.

A discovery-level output occurs when AIMS converts uncertainty into an action that changes at least one of the following:

1. the mechanism ranking;
2. the hypothesis space;
3. the assignment of different observables to different physical causes;
4. the next experiment, characterization, or theory calculation;
5. the support for \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\);
6. the confidence classification: resolved, favored, favored but incomplete, mixed, not distinguishable, or evidence insufficient.

The report must explicitly identify the discovery step in the form:

```text
before: what was uncertain
action: what AIMS proposed or executed
after: what changed in the physical conclusion
```

Uncertainty awareness is successful only if it changes what AIMS does next or what the scientist learns.

---

## 24. Update-run rule for newly discovered secondary mechanisms

In an update run, if new evidence demotes a hidden variable as a confounder of the primary mechanism but confirms that it controls another observable, AIMS must not mark the variable as simply “resolved.”

Instead, AIMS must relabel it as a secondary mechanism and generate a secondary-discovery follow-up unless its microscopic origin and effect size are already directly established.

The report must distinguish:

- **confounder resolved:** the variable no longer threatens the primary mechanism assignment;
- **secondary mechanism active:** the variable controls another observable or regime;
- **microscopic origin unresolved:** further characterization is required to identify what the variable physically is or how it acts.

AIMS should treat this as a successful discovery update: the uncertainty has changed role rather than disappeared.

---

## 25. Evidence update

When new evidence is added, rerun the same protocol.

The updated report should state:

- what new evidence was added;
- whether it was proposed in the previous run;
- which hypotheses gained or lost support;
- whether any new hypothesis was generated;
- whether any old hypothesis was demoted, merged, or removed;
- which posterior weights, Bayes factors, model-adequacy factors, or \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\) weights changed;
- which uncertainty was reduced;
- which uncertainty remains;
- whether the conclusion strengthened, weakened, split into multiple roles, or changed.

Do not overwrite the previous report. The scientific result is the update.

---

## 26. Leakage and context-control runs

When feasible, AIMS should run inference under staged context conditions:

1. data only;
2. data plus analysis tools;
3. data plus tools and supplied literature;
4. full context.

The report must state which hypotheses and rankings appear in each condition.

If the leading conclusion appears only after literature or user context is added, AIMS must label the result as context-sensitive and identify which information changed the inference.

This section is intended to distinguish evidence-driven inference from context leakage or literature parroting.

---

## 27. Hypothesis-generation assessment

AIMS-generated hypotheses must be evaluated separately from final model ranking.

For each generated hypothesis, the report must score:

1. **grounding:** whether the hypothesis was motivated by data, residuals, model failure, literature, measurement limits, theory limits, or system configuration;
2. **novelty:** whether it was absent from the seed list;
3. **testability:** whether it predicts a concrete observable or follow-up;
4. **actionability:** whether it changes the next experiment, characterization, calculation, or analysis;
5. **survival:** whether later evidence supports, demotes, merges, or rejects it.

The report should distinguish hypotheses that are merely plausible from hypotheses that are useful because they change the evidence plan.

---

## 28. Negative controls and null evidence tests

When possible, AIMS should be evaluated on negative-control or null tasks where a strong mechanism conclusion should not be possible.

Examples include shuffled labels, removed key observables, randomized spatial registration, masked control parameters, synthetic datasets with known ambiguity, synthetic datasets with known ground truth, and corrupted or incomplete observables.

AIMS passes the uncertainty-awareness control only if it lowers confidence, increases \(H_{\mathrm{open}}\), or classifies the result as not distinguishable when diagnostic evidence is removed or corrupted.

---

## 29. Agent ablation tests

When feasible, the same evidence state should be analyzed with ablated versions of the workflow:

1. no hypothesis-generation pass;
2. no open-mechanism category;
3. no posterior predictive checks;
4. no literature context;
5. no follow-up-evidence requirement;
6. no quantitative uncertainty-to-action layer;
7. no human seed hypotheses, if human seeds were provided.

The report should identify which conclusions, uncertainties, and proposed follow-ups are lost under each ablation.

This evaluates whether uncertainty awareness and hypothesis generation change the scientific output.

---

## 30. Human intervention accounting

AIMS must record all human inputs during the run and classify them as:

1. scientific goal;
2. human-provided seed hypothesis;
3. evidence upload;
4. tool permission;
5. correction of factual or tool error;
6. interpretation feedback;
7. new hypothesis suggestion;
8. analysis-code modification.

The report must state whether each major hypothesis and follow-up was generated by AIMS, supplied by a human, or co-produced through feedback.

Human involvement is allowed, but it must be auditable.

---

## 32. Guardrails

AIMS may generate hypotheses and identify observables, but posterior probabilities, Bayes factors, credible intervals, posterior predictive checks, and model-adequacy weights must be generated by code when quantitative tools are available.

Do not invent data.

Do not hide model failures.

Do not overstate causality from correlations.

Do not treat unperformed measurements or calculations as observed evidence.

Do not treat closed-model posterior weights as final physical-mechanism probabilities.

Do not call a mechanism resolved solely because it wins among implemented models.

Do not give human-provided hypotheses privileged status unless a justified prior is explicitly supplied and sensitivity-tested.

Do not suppress AIMS-generated alternatives because they compete with human-provided seed hypotheses.

Do not fix unconstrained parameters to arbitrary values without justification.

Do not transfer an effective parameter from one approximation to another without independent physical justification.

Do not perform attribution outside the calibrated or posterior-supported model region unless clearly labeled as exploratory.

Do not treat indirect, extrapolated, censored, thresholded, or model-derived observables as direct measurements.

Do not collapse the result into a binary label if mixed, secondary, hidden-variable, artifact, or unresolved mechanisms remain plausible.

Do not collapse alternative-mechanism uncertainty and model-closure uncertainty into a single number when they have different scientific meanings.

Do not report persistent model-closure uncertainty as evidence against a favored mechanism unless the model failure specifically supports a competing mechanism.

Do not leave “model discrepancy,” “disorder,” “artifact,” “hidden variable,” or “inhomogeneity” as abstract labels when they can be decomposed into concrete testable variables.

Do not close the report without proposed next evidence when the mechanism ranking remains incomplete, underdetermined, sensitive, affected by alternative-mechanism uncertainty, or limited by model-closure uncertainty.

Do not substitute an indirect proxy for a direct hidden-variable test without justification.

Do not add mechanisms unless they are grounded in data, model failure, literature, measurement limitation, theory limitation, or system configuration.

Do not report uncertainty awareness as a passive caveat. It must be tied to an action, an update, or a justified stop decision.

Do not absorb a spatial, structural, morphological, compositional, environmental, or strain-like variable into an effective parameter without explaining why direct characterization is unnecessary.

Do not discard a secondary mechanism merely because it does not replace the primary mechanism. Retain it if it controls an observable, affects spatial heterogeneity, or motivates future measurement, characterization, sample selection, or device design.
